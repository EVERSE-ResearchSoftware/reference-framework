## 2.5 Community

Research software development and maintenance increasingly depend on active communities of contributors, users, and stakeholders. A thriving community can compensate for technical limitations, ensure long-term viability, and drive innovation through collaborative development. For research software, community engagement takes on particular importance due to the distributed nature of research collaborations, the need for domain-specific expertise, and the challenges of sustaining software beyond individual research projects.

Community health encompasses the social and organisational structures that enable effective collaboration, knowledge sharing, and decision-making around research software. This includes governance models, contributor engagement patterns, communication channels, and the coordination processes that facilitate participation across diverse stakeholder groups. The significance of community aspects varies by software tier - analysis code may have a single author with minimal community interaction, while research software infrastructure requires structured community governance and broad stakeholder engagement.

Community aspects overlap with sustainability and open source dimensions, as healthy communities directly contribute to long-term software viability and align with open source development practices. Despite this overlap, community merits explicit attention as it captures critical social dynamics that determine whether research software achieves adoption, maintains quality, and evolves to meet changing research needs.

Key aspects of community health for research software include:

**Community engagement and participation**

- Possible indicators:

  - "has active contributors beyond core maintainers"

  - "responds to issues and pull requests within defined timeframes"

  - "documents contribution activity publicly"

  - "shows growth or stability in user and contributor base"

**Governance structures and processes**

- Possible indicators:

  - "governance model is documented and publicly accessible"

  - "decision-making processes are transparent"

  - "roles and responsibilities are clearly defined"

  - "succession planning exists for key roles"

**Communication channels and practices**

- Possible indicators:

  - "maintains active communication channels (mailing lists, forums, chat)"

  - "provides clear pathways for users to get support"

  - "documents decisions and discussions publicly"

  - "communicates releases and major changes effectively"

**Community health metrics**

- Possible indicators:

  - "tracks community health using established frameworks (e.g., CHAOSS metrics)"

  - "monitors contributor diversity and inclusion"

  - "measures user engagement and satisfaction"

  - "identifies and addresses community bottlenecks"

**Integration with research communities**

- Possible indicators:

  - "engages with domain-specific research communities"

  - "participates in relevant conferences and workshops"

  - "maintains connections with related projects"

  - "facilitates knowledge exchange across research groups"

The CHAOSS (Community Health Analytics Open Source Software) project provides comprehensive frameworks for measuring community health, including 21 software-specific metrics that can be adapted for research software contexts. These metrics offer valuable insights into community sustainability, contributor activity, and project health that complement traditional software quality measures.

### 2.5.1 Good enough practices

This section captures good practices for building and maintaining healthy research software communities identified through EVERSE project activities.

- **Contributor guidelines**: clear documentation of how to contribute code, report issues, and participate in decision-making
- **Code of conduct**: explicit community standards that promote inclusive and respectful collaboration
- **Onboarding processes**: structured approaches to help new contributors and users engage effectively, including tutorials, mentoring, and example use cases
- **Communication infrastructure**: maintained channels (mailing lists, forums, chat platforms) with documented purposes and participation guidelines
- **Recognition practices**: acknowledgement of diverse contributions beyond code, including documentation, user support, and community building
- **Governance documentation**: transparent description of decision-making structures, from benevolent dictator for life (BDFL) models to community committees or sponsor-driven approaches
- **Community roadmaps**: publicly maintained plans that communicate project direction and invite community input
- **Regular community interactions**: workshops, user meetings, developer sprints, or other forums that strengthen community connections
- **Responsive issue management**: timely responses to community questions, bug reports, and feature requests that demonstrate active engagement
- **Diversity and inclusion efforts**: intentional practices to welcome contributors from diverse backgrounds and career stages

### 2.5.2 Examples of tools relevant for community health

The following is a non-exhaustive list of tools that can help assess or improve research software community health. For more comprehensive and up-to-date guidance on tools and services that support community development, see the Research Software Quality Toolkit (RSQKit) and the EVERSE Tech Radar.

**Community health analytics**

- CHAOSS GrimoireLab (https://chaoss.github.io/grimoirelab/) - analytics platform for software development metrics
- Augur (https://github.com/chaoss/augur) - metrics and insights about open source communities
- GitHub Insights (https://docs.github.com/en/communities) - built-in community health analytics for GitHub repositories

**Communication platforms**

- Discourse (https://www.discourse.org/) - open source discussion platform
- Gitter (https://gitter.im/) - chat platform integrated with code repositories
- Slack (https://slack.com/) - team communication (with considerations for long-term accessibility)
- Mailing list tools: Mailman, Google Groups, for asynchronous community discussions

**Contributor onboarding and documentation**

- Read the Docs (https://readthedocs.org/) - documentation hosting with community contribution workflows
- Diataxis (https://diataxis.fr/) - documentation framework supporting diverse user needs
- Good First Issue (https://goodfirstissue.dev/) - discovering contribution opportunities

**Governance and decision-making**

- Community Rule (https://communityrule.info/) - templates and frameworks for community governance
- Open Source Guides (https://opensource.guide/leadership-and-governance/) - guidance on project leadership

**Recognition and credit**

- All Contributors (https://allcontributors.org/) - recognising diverse contribution types
- CRediT (Contributor Roles Taxonomy) - standardised contribution roles

Note that community health is deeply interconnected with sustainability and open source practices. Effective community development requires not just tools but also intentional practices, inclusive cultures, and sustained engagement that evolve with the research software and its user base.
